A listening guide to fourier_partial

Every sound is a chord

Even a single note. Even a slammed door. This is a program that pulls a recording apart into the separate tones hiding inside it, so you can hear them one at a time — and put them back together again.

No maths required Nothing to know in advance Bring headphones

Part one · the idea

You already do this without trying

Put on a record. Somewhere in the room, one loudspeaker is moving back and forth. One moving object. It produces a single, wobbling change in air pressure — and out of that one wobble you hear a bass line, a voice, a cymbal, and the hum of the room they recorded in.

Your ears took one tangled signal and pulled it into separate things. You didn't decide to. You can't switch it off.

That separation is what this program does — but slowly, visibly, and with a pause button. Instead of happening inside your head in an instant, it happens on a screen where you can look at each piece, listen to it alone, turn it off, or save it to a file.

Listen · 1

One sound, or three?

Press All together. That's one sound — a chord. Now press the three coloured buttons and hear what it was made of the whole time.

Sound starts when you press a button — browsers require that.

Nothing was added or removed. The chord is those three tones, happening at once. Playing them together and playing the chord are the same physical event.

The name. Two hundred years ago a French mathematician called Joseph Fourier was studying how heat spreads through metal, and worked out that any complicated wobble can be built from simple, regular ones added together. That idea is now called the Fourier transform, and it is doing the work here.

You do not need to know anything else about it. Everything on this page works whether or not you ever think about that word again.

Part two · the building block

The simplest possible sound

There is one sound that cannot be pulled apart any further. It's a pure tone — a single steady pitch with nothing else in it. A tuning fork is close. A test tone is exactly it.

Every other sound in the world can be built by stacking pure tones on top of each other. Change how many, and how loud each one is, and you change what the sound is — a flute, a violin, a voice, a bell.

Stack some yourself.

Listen · 2

Build a sound out of pure tones

The first slider is the note you'd name. The rest sit above it, and they're what makes it sound like something rather than a test tone.

Leave only the first slider up and you get that thin electronic beep. Raise the others and it thickens into something you could imagine an instrument making — same note, completely different character.

Musicians usually call this additive synthesis: making sounds by adding pure tones together. This program runs it backwards. You hand it a finished sound, and it works out which sliders were up.

Part three · what the program does

A prism for sound

Hold a glass prism up to daylight and a white beam comes out the other side as a spread of colours. The colours were always there. The prism only separates them.

This program is that, for sound. A recording goes in. What comes out is a stack of separate tones — each one on its own track, each one something you can hear by itself.

Part four · the honest part

Not everything is a tone

Pluck a guitar string and you get a note — but you also get the click of the pick, the scrape of a finger, the thump of the body. None of that is a pitch. There's no note you could name for it.

So when the program pulls a recording apart, it puts every steady tone on its own track, and everything else — the breath, the scrape, the hiss of the room — into one last track called the leftover.

Listen · 4

The tone, the noise, and both

A plucked string, taken apart into its two halves.

Only the tones sounds oddly synthetic — that's the pluck missing. Only the leftover is the pluck with the note removed.

This isn't a shortcoming to apologise for. It turns out you genuinely cannot describe a sharp click as a small set of steady tones — a click is over too quickly to have a pitch. The leftover track is where that material has to live.

For most people the leftover is the interesting one. It's the sound of the thing being played rather than the note it produced: breath, bow hair, fingers, the room. Solo it and you get an oddly intimate recording of a performance with all the music taken out.

Part five · for musicians

What you can actually make with it

Everything above is the explanation. This is the part that might change what you do on a Tuesday afternoon.

Resynthesise a field recording

Feed in a recording of a room, a train, a fridge. Keep three tones, throw away the rest, and you have a drone that is literally made of that place — not a filter sweep pretending to be.

Steal one partial from a bell

Struck metal is full of tones that don't belong to any scale. Solo one and export it. You now have a tuning that came from an object rather than from a keyboard.

Turn a sample into MIDI that bends

Export any track as MIDI and it keeps its slides and vibrato as pitch bend, not as a note snapped to the nearest semitone. A vocal glide stays a glide.

Subtractive editing on a source

Mute the two tones muddying a sample and export what's left. This is EQ that removes a specific sounding thing, not a frequency range containing it.

Keep only the leftover

Mute every tone and export the noise track. Bow scrape, valve clatter, breath, room. A percussion library made from the parts of a performance nobody records on purpose.

Find out what a chord actually is

Analyse a sound you like and read the note names off the screen. Useful when something is beautiful and you have no idea why.

The material matters more than the settings. Sustained, pitched things — strings, voice, drones, feedback, bells — pull apart beautifully. Drums, noise and dense mixes mostly land in the leftover track, because there isn't much steady pitch in them to find.

That isn't the program failing. It's telling you something true about the sound.

Part six · using it

What you'll see

Drag an audio file onto the window. It thinks for a while — a few seconds for a short phrase, about a minute for ten minutes of music — and then you get a list.

[M] [S] C4 + 9 harmonics ╱╱╲╲╱╱╲╲╱╱╲╲ [wav] [mid]
[M] [S] E4 + 4 harmonics ╲╲╱╱╲╲╱╱╲╲╱╱ [wav] [mid]
[M] [S] Leftover ▓▒░▒▓░▒▓▒░▓▒ [wav]
  • M mutes a track — everything else keeps playing.
  • S solos it — you hear only that.
  • The name is the note it found, in ordinary note names.
  • The squiggle is that tone's pitch drawn over time. A wobble is vibrato; a slope is a slide.
  • wav saves it as audio. mid saves it as notes for an instrument.

Play everything at once and you hear your original file back. Start muting, and you're editing the sound by its contents rather than by its frequencies.

Two things worth knowing

Long recordings show fewer tracks than they found. Ten minutes of music might contain a thousand notes. You'll get the most prominent forty, and the rest gets folded into the leftover. It'll tell you when it does that. Nothing is lost — you still hear all of it — those notes just don't each get their own row.

Short is sharper. A few seconds of one sound gives a much finer result than ten minutes of a full arrangement. If something matters, cut it down and feed it in on its own.

Part seven · if you're curious

The part with the maths in it

You can stop here and use the program perfectly well. But if any of that raised a question, here are the answers, kept short.

Why can't it separate two instruments playing the same note?

Because it separates by pitch, not by instrument. Two things playing the same note are, as far as the air is concerned, one louder thing. Nothing in the signal says where a tone came from.

Splitting a mix into vocals, drums and bass is a different job that needs a trained model, and this program doesn't attempt it.

Why does a drum recording end up almost entirely in the leftover?

A tone needs time to exist. To be a pitch, something has to repeat regularly for long enough to count the repeats. A drum hit is over before that can happen, so there's no steady pitch to find.

This isn't a limitation of the program. There's a genuine mathematical result saying a sound cannot be both very brief and very precisely pitched — you can have one or the other. It's the same idea as the uncertainty principle in physics, and it's the single biggest constraint on how this program works.

What does "+ 6 harmonics" mean next to a note?

When something makes a note, it doesn't produce one pure tone — it produces a whole family at once: the note you'd name, plus quieter tones at two times, three times, four times its speed. Those are harmonics, and their particular mix is what makes a clarinet sound unlike a violin.

The program gathers a note's harmonics onto one track, because you almost always want to move the note around as a whole. You can still open it up and take them individually.

Is anything lost when it takes a sound apart?

No. This was the central design decision. The leftover track isn't estimated — it's calculated by subtracting the tones from the original, which means the pieces add back up to exactly what you put in. Every sample of it.

Saving a track as audio is likewise lossless. Saving as MIDI is not, and can't be: MIDI describes notes, not sound.

Where did this come from?

Fourier published the underlying idea in 1822, working on heat. It now underpins a startling amount of daily life — every MP3, every phone call, every JPEG, every MRI scan.

It's also still live mathematics. A 2026 result by Alex Cohen, then a doctoral student, sharpened a related principle about how concentrated a signal can be in time and in pitch at once — the same trade-off that decides what this program can and cannot pull apart.
