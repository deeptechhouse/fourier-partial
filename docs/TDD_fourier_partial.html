01What this is

The product in one page: the promise, the audience, and the one sentence everything else serves.

When you listen to a chord, your ear does something remarkable without being asked: it separates one pressure wave into distinct pitches. Quanta Magazine's introduction to the Fourier transform opens on exactly this — as we listen to a piece of music, our ears perform a calculation — and compares it to sniffing a perfume and naming its ingredients.

fourier_partial does that calculation where you can see it. You give it a short phrase; it gives you back the constituent tones as separate tracks you can look at, solo, and export.

What a user does

  1. Drops in 5–30 seconds of audio.
  2. Watches it resolve into a stack of tracks, each a single evolving tone.
  3. Solos one and hears a pure pitch with the original's vibrato and decay intact.
  4. Unmutes everything and hears their recording, exactly.
  5. Exports any track as .wav, or as .mid to rebuild in an instrument.

The one sentence

sum(tracks) == original  —  exactly, always, for every input

Everything in this document either implements that promise or explains a trade-off made to keep it. Section 7 shows why it holds by arithmetic rather than by good engineering, which is what makes it worth promising at all.

Who it is for

AudienceWhat they want from it
Sound designersPull a texture apart, mute what muddies it, re-export the rest
ProducersTurn a sampled tone into MIDI that keeps its glides and vibrato
StudentsSee a Fourier transform do something audible rather than symbolic
Instrument buildersRead the harmonic recipe of a real sound to model it

Explicitly out of scope

  • Stem separation. This is not Demucs. It does not split vocals from drums; it splits frequencies from frequencies.
  • Full songs. Audio up to 10 minutes is supported, but the interface is still built for phrases: a long recording surfaces its 40 most prominent notes and folds the rest into the residual. See section 14.
  • Real-time processing. Analysis is a background job measured in seconds.
  • Transcription. MIDI export describes partials, not a score.

02Why this is hard

A theorem sits at the centre of this system. Most of the parameter choices in section 4 are negotiations with it, and one of the product's best features falls out of it.

A Fourier transform tells you which frequencies a signal contains. It says nothing about when. For music that is useless, so we chop the audio into short windows and transform each one — the Short-Time Fourier Transform.

Immediately there is a problem. Short windows tell you accurately when things happened but poorly what pitch they were. Long windows tell you the pitch precisely but smear the timing. You cannot fix this by being clever. It is the Heisenberg–Gabor limit: the product of time spread and frequency spread has a floor.

Δt · Δf  ≥  1 / 4π

Drag the window size in the panel below and watch it enforce itself. The signal is fixed: two steady tones 15 Hz apart, plus a single sharp click halfway through. No window setting shows you both clearly.

Theory panel — where the residual comes from

Partial tracking assumes the signal is locally a handful of slowly-varying sine waves — that is, sparse in frequency. An uncertainty principle says a signal cannot be sparse in time and in frequency at once. A click is maximally concentrated in time, so its energy must be spread across all frequencies, and no finite set of partials can represent it.

So the residual track is not the leftovers of a mediocre analyser. It is where the mathematics says that material has to go. That reframing has a product consequence: the residual is presented as a first-class track with its own character — the breath, the bow noise, the stick hit — not as a greyed-out error bucket.

The 2026 result, and an honest caveat

The classical limit has been sharpened. Quanta reported in August 2026 that Alex Cohen, then a doctoral student at MIT, proved the fractal uncertainty principle in all higher dimensions — extending the one-dimensional case established by Jean Bourgain and Semyon Dyatlov in 2016. The result appeared in the Annals of Mathematics. The principle says a function cannot be porous — fractal-like, full of gaps — in both physical space and frequency space at once.

We are not going to code against this. It would be dishonest to claim otherwise. What it offers is a qualitative expectation worth stating in the product: sounds whose spectra are gappy and inharmonic — bells, cymbals, struck metal — are exactly the ones that resist clean localisation, and are where partial tracking should be expected to degrade. Section 12 turns that into a test.

Analogy, not application — stated as such

Cohen's proof required defining line porosity, to exclude fractals containing uninterrupted lines — those break the principle. A harmonic series is an evenly spaced arithmetic progression: the “line” of the frequency axis. And harmonic sounds are precisely the ones this tool handles beautifully.

The structured case that breaks the theorem is the structured case that makes the tool work. That is a resonance worth noticing, not a proof of anything, and no engineering decision rests on it.

03Architecture

Ports and adapters. The domain knows nothing about FastAPI, libsndfile, or the browser.

Python does the analysis; the browser does visualisation and playback. That split is not aesthetic — it follows from section 9, where sample-accurate multi-track sync turns out to be nearly free in Web Audio and awkward everywhere else.

The dependency rule

Arrows point inward, always. core imports nothing from the project. If you ever find from backend.analysis import ... inside core/, an arrow has been reversed and the design has quietly stopped being the design.

What is swappable, and why that is cheap

Every replaceable concern sits behind a typing.Protocol in backend/core/interfaces.py. Structural typing means an adapter satisfies a contract without inheriting from anything — so a fake in a test is a plain class with two methods, and a real replacement is no harder.

04Analysis pipeline

Waveform in, partials out. Five stages, every parameter named and defaulted.

# backend/analysis/pipeline.py
audio_io.loader     →  mono float32 @ 44.1 kHz, validated
analysis.stft       →  magnitude + phase, per frame
analysis.peaks      →  sub-bin-accurate spectral peaks
analysis.tracking   →  peaks linked into partials across time
analysis.grouping   →  partials clustered into playable tracks
synthesis           →  each track rendered; residual = original − Σrendered

4.1  Ingest

Decode, downmix to mono, resample to 44.1 kHz. Three decisions worth recording:

  • Mono by averaging. Stereo analysis doubles the partial count and adds a phase-cancellation class of bug. Deferred to section 14.
  • Anti-aliased resampling (scipy.signal.resample_poly, on the gcd-reduced ratio). Cheap resampling folds energy above the new Nyquist limit back down into the audible band as aliases — and an alias is a spectral peak that looks completely real and is not. This tool's whole job is to point at a peak and say “there is a tone here”, so aliasing is the worst possible failure mode: confidently wrong. resample_poly applies a proper FIR anti-aliasing filter as part of the conversion, so those ghosts never form.
  • No normalisation of quiet input. Relative amplitudes are meaningful and rescaling would distort the energy diagnostics. Only clip-hot input is scaled down.

4.2  STFT

Blackman-Harris window, 4096 samples, hop 512 (8× overlap), zero-padded 4×.

Changed during implementation — and why

This originally specified a Hann window. Building it proved that wrong. Hann's first sidelobe sits at −31.5 dB, and the peak threshold is −60 dB relative — so every loud partial grew a pair of phantom neighbours about a semitone away, loud enough to pass the threshold, stable enough to survive tracking, and long enough to survive the duration filter. They arrived in the interface as tracks with confident note names. A pure 440 Hz sine produced eleven partials, ten of them window artefacts.

Blackman-Harris puts its first sidelobe at −92.2 dB, 30 dB below the threshold, so they cannot be picked at all. The same sine now produces exactly one. The cost is a main lobe twice as wide, so close tones need more separation — tests/test_windowing.py pins that a major third still resolves.

For a tool whose promise is “every track is a real tone”, inventing tones is a far worse failure than merging two close ones.

Zero-padding adds no real resolution — that is fixed by window length, as section 2 showed — but it interpolates the spectrum so the peak-finder in 4.3 has a smoother curve to work with.

Frames are timestamped at the window's centre, not its start. The energy sits in the middle; using the start would shift every trajectory earlier by half a window.

4.3  Peak picking

A bin is a candidate if it exceeds both neighbours. Candidates below −60 dB relative to the loudest bin in that frame are discarded — relative, so quiet passages are still analysed rather than ignored.

Then the important part. Bins are 10.8 Hz apart, so a 440 Hz tone does not land on one; it falls between bins 40 and 41. Taking the taller bin rounds it to 431 or 442 Hz — audibly out of tune, and enough jitter to fragment tracking. Fitting a parabola through the peak and its neighbours in dB recovers the true frequency to within a few cents.

4.4  Partial tracking

Peaks per frame are still just snapshots. Tracking links them into trajectories — McAulay–Quatieri, 1986. Each frame, every peak is matched to an existing partial or starts a new one:

EventConditionAction
BIRTHPeak matches no partialStart a new partial
CONTINUEWithin 60 cents of a partial's last frameExtend the trajectory
DEATHUnmatched for > 3 framesClose the partial out

Distance is measured in cents, not Hz. A 10 Hz drift is enormous at 100 Hz and inaudible at 5 kHz; the ear hears ratios, so the tracker must too. A single Hz tolerance would track bass rigidly and treble not at all.

Gap tolerance exists because a partial can be briefly masked by a louder neighbour. Killing it instantly would shatter one sustained note into a dozen fragments, each too short to survive the 50 ms minimum — so a real note would silently become residual noise. Watch it happen:

Set gap tolerance to 0 and run again: the vibrato partial fragments, and fragments get discarded.

Nothing is ever lost

Partials shorter than the minimum are discarded here — but they do not disappear from the output. Because the residual is computed downstream as an exact subtraction, everything discarded at any stage reappears in the residual track automatically. The user still hears it. Section 7.

05Data model

Implemented in full. Immutable value objects, entities with private state, and defensive copies at every boundary.

Peak          frame_index, frequency_hz, amplitude, phase_rad
   ↓ linked across frames by the tracker
PartialFrame  time_s, frequency_hz, amplitude, phase_rad
   ↓ accumulated into a trajectory
Partial       partial_id, frames[]  →  duration, mean_frequency, energy
   ↓ clustered by the grouper
Track         track_id, kind, label, partials[], audio
   ↓ assembled with provenance
AnalysisResult  sample_rate, duration, tracks[], params, original

Encapsulation, and why it is not ceremony

An AnalysisResult is expensive to produce and is read by the API, the exporters, and the verifier. If any of them could mutate a trajectory, the reconstruction invariant would become unverifiable — you could not tell whether an error came from the renderer or from something scribbling on the result afterwards. So:

  • Partial.frames returns a tuple, not the internal list.
  • Track.audio returns a numpy view with writeable = False.
  • Track.attach_audio() raises if called twice — audio is set once, by the renderer.
  • append_frame() enforces strict time ordering, so a trajectory cannot be built out of order.
  • AnalysisParams is frozen, so a result can always report exactly what produced it.

Amplitude-weighted mean frequency

Partial.mean_frequency_hz weights by amplitude rather than averaging plainly. The pitch you perceive is the pitch the partial held while it was loud; a long quiet tail that drifts should not drag the label. This is the number that becomes the track's note name and its MIDI note, so getting it wrong is visible twice.

Fractional MIDI notes

hz_to_midi() deliberately returns a float. 60.5 means “halfway between C4 and C♯4”, which section 10 turns into a note plus a pitch bend. Rounding here would throw away exactly the microtonal detail that makes partials worth looking at.

Wire format

Track metadata goes to the browser as JSON; audio goes separately as binary. Trajectories are downsampled to display resolution before sending — a 20-second track has ~1700 points and the row is ~800 pixels wide, so sending full resolution is pure waste. 

{
  "track_id": 3,
  "kind": "harmonic_group",

06Grouping

A 20-second phrase yields 300–800 partials. A flat list of 800 rows is not an interface; it is a wall.

Two problems, one solution. First, volume: no one can operate 800 controls. Second, and more interesting: an individual partial is often not what a user means by a sound. The 3rd harmonic of a cello note is not a thing anyone wants to solo. The note is.

Algorithm

  1. Rank partials by energy.
  2. Take the strongest ungrouped partial; treat its frequency as a candidate fundamental f0.
  3. Claim every ungrouped partial within 50 cents of n·f0, for n up to 16.
  4. If 3 or more were claimed, emit a HARMONIC_GROUP. Otherwise emit the partial alone as a PARTIAL.
  5. Repeat until every partial is grouped or max_tracks is reached.

A silent cap would be a lie

Partials beyond max_tracks are dropped from the track list. They still sound — the residual absorbs them — but they are no longer separately soloable. The UI must say so explicitly. An interface that shows 200 tracks when 340 were found, without comment, reads as “here is everything” when it is not.

Known weaknesses, stated up front

CaseWhat happensMitigation
Octave-related notesA2 and A3 sounding together: A3's partials are a subset of A2's harmonics, so they may merge into one groupOnset-time agreement as a secondary grouping cue — Phase 2b
Inharmonic sourcesBells and struck metal have stretched partials; harmonic grouping largely fails and they stay as individual PARTIAL tracksAcceptable. Individual partials of a bell are genuinely interesting to solo
Dense polyphonyHarmonics collide; a partial may be claimed by the wrong fundamentalEnergy-ordered greedy claiming means the loudest voice wins, which matches perception
Strong vibratoHarmonics move together; tolerance in cents handles this correctly by constructionNone needed

Interface consequence

Groups fold. A harmonic group shows one row with its label and combined waveform; expanding it reveals the constituent partials as indented rows, each independently soloable. That keeps the common case at ~15 rows and the detailed case reachable.

07Resynthesis and the invariant

The most important section. Everything else is quality; this is correctness.

7.1  Rendering a partial

Each partial is a recipe: at time t, oscillate at f(t) with amplitude a(t). Two things go wrong if you are careless:

  • Clicks. Jump the amplitude at frame boundaries and you get 86 clicks per second, which sounds like buzzing, not music. Interpolate amplitude linearly across each hop.
  • Phase tearing. Restart the oscillator's phase each frame and the waveform rips. Accumulate phase continuously: phase += 2πf[n]/sr.

McAulay–Quatieri fit a cubic polynomial to phase. Linear frequency interpolation with accumulated phase is simpler, nearly as good, and much easier to get right. Start there; measure before reaching for the cubic.

7.2  The residual, and why the promise holds

Here is the whole design decision. Rather than modelling the noise, or filtering for what the partials missed, the residual is defined as a literal time-domain subtraction:

residual  :=  original  −  Σ rendered partials

Therefore, by simple rearrangement:

Σ all tracks  ≡  original

Not approximately. Identically. And critically: this holds regardless of how well the analysis performed. Every partial could be mistracked, the grouping could be nonsense, and the sum would still reproduce the upload sample for sample. Analysis quality determines how musically meaningful each track is; it never determines whether the mix reconstructs.

That removes an entire class of risk from the project. The headline claim cannot be broken by a bad tracking heuristic, only by an arithmetic bug — which section 12 asserts against in CI.

Implementation note that matters

Accumulate the sum in float64 and cast once at the end. Summing several hundred float32 buffers accrues rounding error that shows up as an audible noise floor in the residual — a bug that looks like a DSP failure and is actually a numerics one.

The bug this design hid, and what it cost

The invariant is powerful but it is also anaesthetic: it holds even when the analysis is badly wrong, so it cannot tell you that anything is broken. The first working build reported a perfect 2.98×10−8 reconstruction error while quietly producing garbage.

The cause was a phase-reference mismatch. A DFT reports phase relative to sample 0 of its input, but frames are timestamped at the window centre — half a window apart, 46 ms at the defaults. Every rendered partial came out at exactly the right frequency and exactly the right amplitude and exactly anti-phase (measured correlation with the original: −0.9987). Subtracting an inverted copy doubles the tone instead of cancelling it, so a pure sine reported 19.8% of its energy as partials when the true answer is ~100%.

The fix is zero-phase windowing (rotate the padded frame so the window centre lands at index 0) plus a π/2 offset to convert the DFT's cosine convention to the renderer's sine. Both are needed; either alone still fails. Afterwards: correlation +0.9982, partial energy 94.6%.

The lesson is about which metric to watch. Reconstruction error was constant across the bug and the fix. Partial energy ratio moved from 19.8% to 94.6% — so that, not the invariant, is the number that tells you the analysis is working. It is now a first-class diagnostic in the interface, and tests/test_windowing.py asserts the phase directly.

7.3  Cost

Rendering is the pipeline's most expensive stage — roughly 35% of total time. A 20-second phrase with 400 partials means 400 × 882,000 sample writes. Vectorised per-partial in numpy this lands in low single-digit seconds; the phase accumulation is the part that resists vectorisation and should be the first thing profiled if it disappoints.

08Visualisation

Two views on one timeline. The default is the one that answers the question users actually have.

8.1  Amplitude view

The familiar envelope. Good for seeing when a track is active; useless for seeing what pitch it is. Available, not default.

8.2  Trajectory view — the default

Frequency plotted against time, with line brightness following amplitude. This is the view that makes a partial legible as a tone: the vibrato is a ripple, the glide is a ramp, the decay is a fade. “Which of these is the one that slides?” is a question the amplitude view cannot answer and this one answers at a glance.

The frequency axis is logarithmic

Pitch perception is logarithmic, so an octave must occupy the same height whether it runs 100–200 Hz or 5–10 kHz. A linear axis crushes every musically interesting partial into the bottom few pixels and devotes most of the canvas to air.

Colour carries frequency

The spectral ramp — violet through cyan to amber — maps to register. A track's colour tells you roughly where it sits before you read its label, which is what makes a 40-row stack scannable. The residual is deliberately neutral grey: it has no single frequency, and colouring it would imply one.

Drawing cheaply

Never draw more points than the canvas has pixels. Downsample by min/max per column. The server already sends display-resolution trajectories (section 5) precisely so the client is not doing this work on megabytes it did not need.

09Playback

Where the architecture's browser/server split is decided.

The approach

The server renders each track to PCM. The browser decodes them into AudioBuffers and schedules every source at one shared AudioContext timestamp: 

const startAt = ctx.currentTime + LOOKAHEAD;
sources.forEach(s => s.start(startAt, offset));

The audio thread honours that timestamp to the sample. Two hundred tracks start on exactly the same sample, every time, regardless of what the main thread is doing. Sync is not something we maintain — it is something we get.

Mute and solo are gain, never transport

Muting sets a GainNode to zero over a 15 ms ramp. The source keeps playing silently, stays in sync, and unmutes instantly. Stopping and restarting sources to mute would resynchronise everything and click — and would make solo feel laggy in exactly the moment users are A/B-ing.

Why this keeps the promise

The backend guarantees Σtracks == original. Playback is a sample-aligned sum of those same buffers at unity gain. So “unmute everything” reproduces the upload exactly — the claim is kept by arithmetic rather than by careful mixing.

Rejected: client-side oscillator bank

Synthesising partials live in the browser with OscillatorNodes was the obvious alternative and is worse on three counts. Two hundred oscillators each carrying per-partial frequency and gain automation curves is fragile under load; the automation timing is not sample-exact, so the mix would only approximately equal the original — abandoning section 7's whole point; and it duplicates the renderer in a second language, so the two would drift.

Memory

200 tracks × 30 s × 44.1 kHz × 4 bytes ≈ 1.06 GB decoded. Not acceptable. Mitigations, in order:

  1. Fetch on demand. Only decode a track's audio when it is first unmuted. The common case touches a handful.
  2. Mixdown for “play all”. Serve one pre-mixed buffer for the everything-on case — which is the original file, so it costs nothing to produce.
  3. Evict least-recently-unmuted above a configured ceiling.

10Export

One lossless path and one lossy one. The lossy one is documented as lossy.

10.1  WAV — lossless

A track's rendered audio is already exactly what the user heard when they soloed it, so exporting is a write. The only decision is bit depth: 24-bit PCM by default (what DAWs expect, ample headroom, half the size of float), with 32-bit float offered for users who intend to re-sum the tracks and want the original's samples back bit for bit.

10.2  MIDI — lossy, and here is exactly how

A partial is a continuous curve of frequency and amplitude. MIDI is discrete events on a 12-tone grid. Converting one to the other must discard information. The job is to discard as little as possible and be honest about the rest.

Partial propertyMIDI representationFidelity
FrequencyNote number + pitch bendExcellent within bend range
Amplitude (initial)Note-on velocity7-bit, quantised
Amplitude (over time)CC 11 expression, every 20 msGood; 7-bit steps
LifetimeNote-on / note-offExact to tick resolution
Phase—Discarded entirely
Timbre—N/A — a partial is a sine

The trap that silently breaks MIDI export

Pitch bend range is a per-channel setting that defaults to ±2 semitones. A partial sweeping an octave clips at the limit and comes out wrong — with no error, just a file that sounds incorrect. So on every channel we use, emit the RPN 0 sequence to widen the range to ±48 semitones before anything else. Forgetting this is the single most common way this feature ships broken.

10.3  The 16-channel wall

Pitch bend applies to a whole channel, not to one note. Two tracks sharing a channel bend each other. MIDI has 16 channels; we routinely have 200 tracks. They cannot coexist in one file.

Export modeOutputNotes
Per track (default)One .mid per trackUnambiguous, unlimited, imports correctly everywhere
BundleOne .zip of per-track filesConvenience wrapper on the above
MPE-styleOne multi-channel .midTop 15 tracks by energy. Channel 10 skipped — GM percussion. Must report what was omitted.

The residual is not MIDI-exportable at all. It is broadband noise; writing it as notes would be fabricating information rather than converting it. Track.is_exportable_as_midi returns False and the UI disables the button rather than failing after the click.

11.1  Why a job, not a synchronous response

Analysing 20 seconds takes seconds, not milliseconds. Holding an HTTP request open for that is fragile — proxies time out — and gives the user nothing to look at. A job id plus a progress socket costs a little plumbing and makes the wait legible.

11.2  Progress weights

Roughly proportional to observed cost on a 20-second phrase:

StageWeightReported as
Peak finding0.30“Finding tones”
Tracking0.25“Following tones through time”
Grouping0.05“Grouping harmonics”
Rendering0.35“Rendering tracks”
Residual0.05“Finishing”

Stage names are written for the user, not the developer. Someone waiting on a progress bar wants to know what is happening to their sound, not which module holds the GIL.

11.3  Job store

In-memory with TTL expiry, deliberately not a database. Analyses are ephemeral by design (section 13): a session's audio is user data we have no reason to retain. Jobs run in a background thread while the event loop keeps serving, so every mutation is lock-guarded — getting that wrong yields the worst class of bug here: intermittent, unreproducible, load-dependent.

If multi-worker deployment ever becomes necessary, InMemoryJobStore is the seam: implement JobStore against Redis and change one line of wiring.

12.2  The invariant assertion

tests/test_reconstruction.py is the acceptance criterion for Phase 2 and a permanent CI gate thereafter. It asserts reconstruction_error() < 1e-6 on tonal input, on noise, and on silence.

The noise case is the important one. Noise produces few usable partials, so nearly everything lands in the residual — and the sum must still be exact. That is the test proving the guarantee comes from the subtraction rather than from good analysis.

12.3  A test derived from section 2

Because the uncertainty principle predicts that tonal material decomposes into partials and transient material does not, that becomes falsifiable: 

def test_partial_capture_ranks_tonal_above_noisy():
    """The ordering is the claim; thresholds are calibration."""
    tonal = _verify(make_harmonic_tone(220.0, 8, duration_s=2.0))
    noisy = _verify(make_noise_burst(duration_s=2.0))
    assert tonal.partial_energy_ratio > noisy.partial_energy_ratio

Absolute thresholds drift as parameters are tuned; the ordering is structural. Asserting the relationship rather than the number gives a test that survives tuning and still catches a genuine regression.

12.4  Coverage honesty

The scaffold's stub modules are listed in pyproject.toml's coverage omit, each annotated with its phase. Delete each line as its phase lands so the 90% gate tightens automatically instead of being quietly forgotten. 24 tests pass today against the implemented domain model.

13Security

The app accepts file uploads from a browser. Treat it as internet-facing whether or not it ever is.

13.1  Threat model

No accounts, no persistence, no secrets held. That narrows the surface a great deal, and leaves three real concerns: malicious uploads, resource exhaustion, and information leakage through errors.

13.2  Upload validation, in order

  1. Size, before reading. 50 MB ceiling. A 4 GB “wav” should never reach memory.
  2. Magic bytes, not the extension. An extension is a claim by the uploader; magic bytes are evidence. _MAGIC_SIGNATURES in audio_io/loader.py.
  3. Duration, from the header, before decoding samples. 30-second ceiling.
  4. Content. Reject empty or wholly silent files — they produce degenerate results and confusing UI.
  5. Storage outside the web root, with generated filenames. User-supplied names are never used on disk.

13.4  Errors never leak internals

Every exception in backend/core/errors.py carries a user_message written for a person. Exception text — which contains paths, parameters, and stack frames — goes to the log and never to the browser. JobStore.fail() documents this explicitly because it is the one place the temptation to pass str(exception) through is strongest.

13.5  Supply chain

All dependencies pinned exactly, no floating ranges. Every package was verified present on PyPI at pin time — a guard against slopsquatting, where a plausible-sounding but nonexistent package name gets registered by an attacker. pip-audit --strict runs in CI and fails the build on any critical or high vulnerability. Licences are all permissive (BSD/ISC/MIT/Apache-2.0), which the open-source goal requires.

13.6  Not applicable

No AI or LLM components, so the agentic-security considerations do not apply. No database, so no injection surface. No authentication, so no broken access control — but note that this is because there is no per-user data; adding accounts later would reopen the whole category.

14.3  Deployment

Local-first: python3 -m backend.main, preferred port 8420, auto-selecting if busy. For a hosted demo, any container host works — the app is stateless apart from the TTL job store. The constraint is CPU and RAM per analysis, not concurrency, so a small always-on instance beats serverless, where cold starts and execution limits fit this workload badly.

14.4  What would make this fail

Recorded honestly, so the risks are visible rather than discovered:

  • Track counts are unusable in practice. Grouping is the mitigation and it is unproven on real polyphony. This is the highest risk in the project.
  • Rendering is too slow. 400 partials × 882k samples has an unpleasant constant factor. Inverse-FFT overlap-add is the fallback.
  • Soloed partials are less interesting than they sound in theory. A single sine is a thin thing to listen to. Harmonic groups are the answer, which raises the stakes on grouping quality again.
  • Browser memory on large analyses. Section 9's mitigations are designed but untested.
